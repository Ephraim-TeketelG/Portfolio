01. The Full Story

I grew up in Shinshicho, a town in Central Ethiopia. It was there, attending St. Comboni School for Grades 7 and 8, that my foundational drive began to take shape. During my eighth-grade year, I took the initiative to tutor my classmates every morning before school to help them prepare for the regional examination. The hard work paid off. At the end of Grade 8, I achieved one of the highest scores in my zone, earning a 94.6 average and placing in the 99.99 percentile.

My deep passion for mathematics truly sparked around Grade 7. I found myself completely captivated by mathematical puzzles, novel discoveries, and exploring complex ideas with my teachers. That curiosity pushed me fast. By Grade 8, I had already started writing my first independent mathematics research paper.

During Grade 9, I took a major step forward by finding a mentor from Kotebe University. My mentor guided me through the writing process and helped me refine my research. This preparation led to a defining moment in Grade 10 when I presented my first research paper at the International Conference on Mathematics and Mathematics Education (ICMME 2025). The conference was hosted by the Ethiopian Mathematics Professionals Association (EMPA) at Dilla University. Following my presentation, I was officially certified and nominated as an EMPA Ambassador.

I remained highly active in EMPA events. In 2026, I presented once again during the International Day of Mathematics (Pi Day) and completed my second independent mathematics research paper. Today, I am still rigorously refining both papers before pursuing official publication.

Beyond academics, leadership remains one of the strongest pillars of my life. I believe success means becoming a better version of myself than I was yesterday and solving real problems through realistic initiatives. Ultimately, I am striving for a world where everyone is treated first as a human being, with dignity, compassion, and respect.

Moral vs. Spiritual Religion

These days I have been stuck thinking about this and I strongly felt that sharing my opinion would be the right approach. Have you ever realized the way you have been raised, especially in the spiritual sense? Was it really a real relationship with God, or was it something closer to moralistic religion?

I genuinely feel that many parents, at least those I have seen, while convincing themselves that they are raising their children spiritually, may actually be creating a difficult or shallow relationship with God for them. It is not intentional. I believe it is mostly misunderstanding. They teach their children about God, morality, what to do and what not to do, make them attend church, and do many religious activities. But later, many of those children end up being unfamiliar with God in a personal sense. They struggle to understand what a real relationship with God looks like. Faith becomes something decent, but distant.

This is not necessarily because they were not raised in a "spiritual" way. It is because they were taught about God, but not introduced to who God is in a lived, relational sense. They learned about Christianity, but not necessarily what it means to actually walk with God.

This made me curious, so I tried to check whether this was just my personal thought or something that has been observed more widely. I looked deeper into it, and I was honestly surprised by how many related ideas already exist. I came across concepts like Moralistic Therapeutic Deism, legalism vs grace in Christianity, the critique of the Pharisees, the idea of the new covenant, and key biblical teachings and verses.

The realization was uncomfortable. It felt like many people in the world may already be caught in this without noticing it. We were raised morally, but not always spiritually. That thought kept repeating in my mind.

Moralistic Therapeutic Deism describes how many people who identify with religion do not actually live out its core beliefs, but instead follow an unofficial belief system. At its core, it reduces faith into something simple and minimal: be a good person, try to be happy, and expect God to help when needed. It sounds harmless, even positive, but it slowly removes depth from spirituality. It turns God into a distant helper rather than the center of life.

Then I looked at legalism vs grace in Christianity. Legalism is the mindset that acceptance from God is earned through obedience. Grace is the idea that acceptance comes first, and obedience flows from that relationship. The shift between these two is not small. It completely changes whether faith becomes a burden of performance or a response of transformation.

This connects strongly to the way Jesus addressed the Pharisees in Matthew 23. The issue was not their discipline or their knowledge of the law, but the gap between their external behavior and their internal condition. Outwardly correct, inwardly disconnected.

The idea of the new covenant in Jeremiah 31:33 goes further. It speaks about a time when God's law is not just outside people as rules, but inside them as something written on their hearts. That suggests spirituality was always meant to become internal, not just behavioral. This is also reflected in the command to love God with all heart, soul, and mind (Deuteronomy 6 and Matthew 22). It shifts the focus from rule-following to relationship, from performance to total inner devotion.

In Galatians 5, the idea of the fruit of the Spirit contrasts with self-effort. Growth is described not as forced behavior but as natural fruit from an inner source of life. That again points away from external pressure and toward internal change.

Modern thinkers also describe this in different ways. Dallas Willard emphasizes spiritual formation as becoming a different kind of person, not just believing differently. Henri Nouwen focuses on identity rooted in being loved rather than performing. Richard Rohr speaks about moving beyond ego-driven faith into deeper maturity. Timothy Keller summarizes it simply as gospel versus religion: acceptance leading to transformation, not transformation earning acceptance.

When I connect all of this, a pattern becomes very clear. There seems to be a consistent tension in spirituality between two directions. One direction is external: rules, behavior, identity based on performance, religion as duty. The other direction is internal: heart transformation, relationship, identity based on grace, and obedience as a result rather than a requirement.

This made me reflect again on what many people, including myself, might have experienced growing up. We may have learned how to act religiously, but not always how to actually know God in a personal and transformative way. And maybe that is the real question I am left with.
